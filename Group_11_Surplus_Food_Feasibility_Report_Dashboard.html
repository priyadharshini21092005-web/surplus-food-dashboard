Survey on Food Wastage at Functions and Events and the Feasibility of a Surplus Food Redistribution Service

Group No. 11 • Members: Shreenidhi.K, Sriramanathanvalliappan Ven, Vaishnavi.L and Yuvaraj.M

Study basis: 33 survey responses • Proposed focus: suitable surplus food from functions and events • Purpose: assess whether a coordinated local redistribution service merits a pilot.

SECTION 1 OF 8

1. Terms of Reference (5W1H)

QuestionApplication to this study
What?Examine the feasibility of collecting suitable surplus food from functions and events and transferring it to verified recipient organisations.
Why?Food can remain after events while community organisations may need food support. A coordinated process may reduce avoidable disposal and improve access, provided safety is protected.
Who?Potential food providers include event organisers and venues. Potential recipients include verified charities, community kitchens, shelters, children’s homes and other eligible organisations. Volunteers or a small coordination team may support transfers.
Where?The service would begin in a limited local area so collection distance, travel time and recipient capacity can be checked before expansion.
When?Collection would be arranged around event completion and only within a safe, agreed time window based on food type, storage and handling conditions.
How?Register providers and recipients, record the food available, confirm suitability, match the donation, arrange rapid collection, document handover and collect feedback.
SECTION 2 OF 8

2. Methodology

This study uses a descriptive survey approach. The supplied questionnaire responses were reviewed for 33 respondents. Counts were converted into percentages using 33 as the base for single-choice questions. For multi-select questions, percentages indicate the share of respondents selecting each option; therefore, percentages for a question may add up to more than 100%.

Scope and limitations of the method

  • The results describe the views of the people who responded; they do not necessarily represent every event organiser, recipient organisation or resident.
  • The survey measures stated opinions and preferences. It does not directly measure kilograms of food saved, the number of successful deliveries, actual costs or food-safety outcomes.
  • No financial quotations or operational trial results are included in the survey figures. Those items must be verified during a pilot.
SECTION 3 OF 8

3. Main findings and merits

  • There is a visible surplus-food problem: 15 respondents (45.5%) reported observing food wastage at almost every event; a further 11 (33.3%) said sometimes.
  • Donation already occurs: 15 respondents (45.5%) said excess food is donated to organisations or people in need. This suggests that some awareness and informal practice may already exist, although arrangements may vary.
  • Over-planning contributes to surplus: overestimation of guest attendance was selected by 21 respondents (63.6%), preparing more food than required by 17 (51.5%), and last-minute guest-number changes by 16 (48.5%).
  • Perceived potential is encouraging: 22 respondents (66.7%) selected “almost all” or “more than half” as potentially redistributable under a proper system.
  • Support is high in this sample: 30 respondents (90.9%) answered definitely or probably yes to supporting or participating in the service.
  • Respondents identify practical safeguards: food-safety and handling checks were selected by 22 respondents (66.7%); timely collection and delivery by 15 (45.5%).
  • Feasibility is conditional: 27 respondents (81.8%) considered it very feasible or feasible with some challenges, while 5 (15.2%) said major improvements would be needed.

Merit: A coordinated service could make matching, collection and handover more predictable, while giving organisers a clear alternative to disposal for food that is still suitable for consumption.

SECTION 4 OF 8

4. Limitations, risks and operational feasibility

Key risks

  • Food safety: unsafe or poorly stored food must not be distributed. Clear acceptance rules, hygiene practices, temperature controls where relevant, traceability and a right to reject unsuitable food are essential.
  • Time sensitivity: collection delays may make food unsuitable. The team must confirm availability and recipient capacity before collection.
  • Supply uncertainty: the amount and timing of surplus food can change from event to event.
  • Transport and storage: travel distance, vehicle availability, packaging and temporary storage can affect cost and safety.
  • Recipient verification: organisations must be checked for legitimacy, need, contact details and ability to accept food at the planned time.
  • Accountability: the provider, coordinator and recipient should know who is responsible at each stage and how a transfer is recorded.

Operational judgement

The idea appears operationally testable on a small scale, but it is not ready to be treated as a fully proven service. A pilot should use pre-registered providers and recipients, one coordinator, a simple communication channel, a written food acceptance checklist and a handover record. Food that fails the safety criteria should be refused rather than redistributed.

SECTION 6 OF 8

6. SWOC analysis

Strengths

  • 90.9% stated positive support.
  • Donation is already a reported outcome for some events.
  • Survey identifies specific trust measures.

Weaknesses

  • Surplus volume and timing are uncertain.
  • Food is time-sensitive and needs careful handling.
  • Actual costs and performance remain untested.

Opportunities

  • Build partnerships with venues and verified organisations.
  • Use a simple phone/WhatsApp-based matching process at first.
  • Track food transferred and recipient feedback.

Challenges

  • Transport and collection delays.
  • Food safety, responsibility and public trust.
  • Irregular supply and uncertain funding.
SECTION 7 OF 8

7. Recommendations

  1. Start small: test the process with a few willing event organisers and a small number of verified recipient organisations.
  2. Set safety rules before launch: define accepted food types, handling conditions, collection deadlines, documentation and refusal criteria using appropriate local food-safety guidance.
  3. Agree the handover process: confirm food quantity, preparation/collection time, storage conditions, recipient, transport and contact person.
  4. Choose a practical coordination method: begin with phone calls or WhatsApp plus a structured log rather than investing immediately in a costly app.
  5. Keep transparent records: record provider, food description and quantity, time offered, collection time, recipient and outcome while protecting personal information.
  6. Measure costs and results: track trips, fuel/transport expenses, volunteer hours, quantity accepted, quantity rejected and successful deliveries.
  7. Secure funding partners: approach potential donors, CSR programmes and community organisations only after the pilot demonstrates a safe, reliable process.
  8. Review after the pilot: expand only if safety, delivery reliability, recipient satisfaction and costs are acceptable.
SECTION 8 OF 8

8. Conclusion

The survey suggests that a surplus-food redistribution service for functions and events deserves a carefully managed pilot. Respondents expressed strong potential support and many believe a substantial amount of excess food could be redistributed with an organised system. However, food safety, collection timing, transport, regular supply, accountability and funding are real constraints. The appropriate conclusion is conditionally feasible: test the idea locally, establish safeguards, collect operational and financial evidence, and use the pilot results to decide whether the service should continue or expand.

Overall decision: Proceed to a limited pilot, not immediate large-scale implementation.
